Episode Six · August 28, 2026

The Peak Always Lands the Same Way

Where a coincidence stops being one · status: proven

Twenty-seven's climb topped out at 9,232.

That number was not free to land anywhere on the number line.

Every peak checked so far has landed in the same narrow slice, and there's a reason.

Episode five ended on a number that seemed arbitrary: 9,232, the highest point twenty-seven reaches before falling back to one. Run the real rule on other odd starting numbers and check where each one peaks. A pattern shows up immediately.

n=7  →  peak 52  ·  n=27  →  peak 9,232

Fifty-two and 9,232 have almost nothing in common on the surface. One is two digits, the other is four. But divide each by six and check the remainder:

52 mod 6 = 4     9,232 mod 6 = 4

Every peak found while writing this episode, across every starting number tried, lands on the same remainder: four, mod six. Not most. Every one. A pattern that consistent stops being interesting as a coincidence and starts being interesting as a thing to prove.

The proof turns out to be short.

Start with what a peak actually is: the single highest value a trajectory ever reaches. Consider how a sequence gets to any point. There are only two ways to arrive somewhere: by halving down from a larger even number, or by climbing up from an odd number through a multiply-and-add-one step. If the peak were reached by halving down, its predecessor would have been twice as large and would have appeared earlier in the same sequence, which is impossible, since the peak is defined as the largest value the whole trajectory ever reaches. So the peak has exactly one way to be reached: it is always the result of a multiply step, applied to some odd number that came right before it.

peak  = 3m + 1  for some odd m

That single fact already answers half the question. Three times an odd number is odd. Odd plus one is even. So the peak, whatever else is true about it, is always even. That rules out three of the six possible remainders mod six before doing anything else.

The second half comes from the same equation. Three times m, plus one, leaves a remainder of exactly one when divided by three, regardless of what m is — three times anything is always a multiple of three, and a multiple of three plus one always lands on remainder one.

peak mod 3  = 1   ·   peak mod 2  = 0

Even, and one more than a multiple of three. Only one remainder mod six satisfies both conditions at once. Not zero mod six, that's a multiple of three. Not two mod six, that's two more than a multiple of three, not one. Four mod six is the only even remainder that also lands one past a multiple of three.

even  and  ≡ 1 (mod 3)  →  the only match is peak ≡ 4 (mod 6)

This holds for every trajectory that climbs above its starting number. A starting number that never climbs, a power of two counting down to one with no multiply steps at all, has no peak in this sense, its highest point is just where it started. For every other odd number, the peak is forced into the same residue class by nothing more than what multiplying by three and adding one does to parity and divisibility.

Fifty-two.

Nine thousand two hundred thirty-two.

Different starting numbers, wildly different scales, the same three-line reason for where each one tops out.

Where it stands

Every peak lands at four mod six. Proven, not just observed, from nothing more than how a multiply-and-add-one step interacts with parity and remainders mod three. What's still open: not every peak is the single highest point of its entire trajectory. Some numbers climb, fall partway, then climb again to a second, different high point. Checking those secondary peaks against the same six-slot grid turns up a second, tighter pattern hiding inside this one.
