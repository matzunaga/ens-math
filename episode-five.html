Episode Five · August 28, 2026

Twenty-Seven

Where the swapping stops · status: computed

Four episodes of substitutes. Time to run the real thing.

No swapped-in Mersenne prime. No heuristic standing in for the leading number. Three, exactly as written, on the number with the worst reputation for cooperating.

Twenty-seven is famous in Collatz circles for taking its time.

Every episode so far tested a stand-in. Seven and thirty-one swapped for three, checking whether the representativeness heuristic could predict a sequence's behavior before running it. Episode four closed that question algebraically. None of it touched the actual, unmodified rule: three times n plus one when n is odd, n divided by two when n is even. Twenty-seven runs that rule, unmodified, starting small and ending nowhere near where it started.

the rule  →  odd n → 3n + 1  ·  even n → n / 2

Twenty-seven takes 111 steps to reach one. That single number undersells what happens along the way.

It doesn't climb steadily.

It overshoots.

By step 77, the sequence peaks at 9,232, a number more than 340 times the starting value, before spending the remaining 34 steps falling all the way back down to one. Twenty-seven doesn't just take longer than seven or thirty-one to resolve. It goes somewhere neither of them went, high enough to make the eventual return to one look less like a foregone conclusion and more like something the sequence had to fight its way back to.

27  →  step 77: peak 9,232  →  step 111: 1

The heuristic from episode two predicts an average of two halvings for every multiply-by-three-plus-one step, since a random odd number times three plus one has, on average, two factors of two to shed before it's odd again. Count the actual steps and the real number comes in lower.

41 odd steps  ·  70 even steps  →  actual average 70 / 41 ≈ 1.71

One point seven one, against a predicted two point zero zero. That's not the heuristic breaking. A coin flipped forty-one times doesn't owe anyone exactly twenty heads, and forty-one halving-runs averaging 1.71 instead of 2.00 is exactly the kind of gap small samples produce on their own, a pattern with its own name: insensitivity to sample size. The heuristic describes what happens on average, over many numbers and many steps. Twenty-seven is one number. Its one trajectory is allowed to sit off the average without the average being wrong.

Real rule.

Real number.

111 steps, a peak 340 times the start, and a return to one that took longer than the climb up. Twenty-seven earned its reputation.

Where it stands

Twenty-seven reaches one, matching every trajectory checked so far, and its 1.71 average sits within the range small samples are expected to produce around a 2.00 heuristic. Nothing here proves the rule always terminates. It's one more computed data point, not a proof about every odd number ever built. What the peak did raise: 9,232 isn't a random-looking number where it landed. The next episode looks at whether that's coincidence.
