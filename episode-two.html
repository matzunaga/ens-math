Episode Two · August 23, 2026

Running the Numbers

Where it picks up · status: computed

The original conversation this continues took place March 2, 2026. This entry was written and computed today, using real code, not a transcript from that night.

This is the story of numbers that were already running.

No one had watched them.

Yet.

Today, someone finally did.

Episode one ended on a heuristic, not a fact: swap the 3 in Collatz for a 7, and the expected multiplier flips from 3/4 to 7/4. Growth instead of contraction. The hand trace agreed, climbing past a million before it got abandoned mid-calculation.

A heuristic can't settle the question. Only the arithmetic can.

n is whatever number you start with.

odd n  →  7n + 1
even n  →  n / 2

Starting from three, run it all the way out:

3  →  22  →  11  →  78  →  39  →  274  →  137  →  960  →  480  →  240  →  120  →  60  →  30  →  15  →  106  →  53  →  372  →  186  →  93  →  652  →  …

The first eight terms match episode one's hand trace exactly. That's confirmation the setup is right. Everything past 960 is new ground.

It doesn't turn back.

By step 5, it's past 100.

By step 22, past 1,000.

By step 34, past 100,000.

By step 42, past a million.

By step 59, past a billion.

By step 86, past a trillion.

step 134: 1,540,236,929,715,296
sixteen digits. Past a quadrillion and a half (a one followed by fifteen zeros).

The run got stopped there, on purpose. A machine has to stop somewhere. The sequence didn't stop with it. Nothing in the climb ever turned around.

That's not proof.

A computer stopping a run isn't the same as a number choosing to change direction.

What's actually on the record is the strongest evidence available so far. It lines up exactly with what the 7/4 expected multiplier predicted, and exactly against what the opening claim about Mersenne primes assumed.

One more thing showed up buried in the count. Forty-eight times the rule multiplied by seven and added one. Eighty-six times it just halved. Almost two halvings for every multiply, close to what the heuristic predicted before a single number got computed. The theory called its shot before the arithmetic ever ran.

A heuristic that called it.

Arithmetic that delivered, sixteen digits of it.

And an opening claim about Mersenne primes, now sitting overturned by its own numbers.

What's known so far

Nobody has proven this sequence never turns back. Nobody has proven it doesn't either. What's on record now is 134 steps and sixteen digits of climbing, with nothing in the data pointing the other way.
