Episode Four · August 28, 2026

Why Three

Where the case closes · status: proven

This is the story of a number that was never actually competing.

Three didn't win by being the best of its family.

It won because it was the only member of that family built to.

Episode three left one line hanging: three might be the only Mersenne prime whose swap-in ever predicts contraction instead of growth. That was an observation sitting on two data points, seven and thirty-one. An observation isn't a proof. Here's the proof.

A Mersenne prime takes the form two to the power of p, minus one, where p itself has to be prime. Three is two squared minus one, p equals two. Seven is two cubed minus one, p equals three. Thirty-one is two to the fifth minus one, p equals five. The heuristic from episode two says the expected multiplier, when that Mersenne prime stands in for the leading three in the rule, is the Mersenne prime divided by four.

expected multiplier  →  (2p − 1) / 4

Contraction means that multiplier has to land below one. Set up the inequality and solve it plainly:

(2p − 1) / 4 < 1
2p − 1 < 4
2p < 5

Two to the power of p has to come in under five. Two to the first power is two. Two squared is four. Two cubed is already eight, past the line for good, and every power after that only climbs. So the exponent has to be one or two for the inequality to hold at all.

That's the first condition.

The second condition is the one that was already sitting in the definition. A Mersenne prime requires p itself to be prime, not just the result. One is not a prime number, by definition, no exceptions. That leaves exactly one candidate that satisfies both conditions at once.

p must be prime  and  p ≤ 2  →  the only prime at or under two is p = 2

p equals two gives two squared minus one, which is three. Nothing else on the prime number line is small enough. Five is prime but 2 to the fifth is 32, nowhere near under five. Seven's exponent alone breaks the inequality before the primality check even matters.

Two requirements.

One overlap.

Three wasn't the strongest performer in a field of contenders. It was the only number that could satisfy both conditions simultaneously. Every other Mersenne prime fails the size test before anyone gets to ask whether it predicts growth or shrinkage — the exponent alone rules it out.

This closes the question episode three opened but leaves a separate one standing, and it matters more than the algebra does. The heuristic predicting contraction for three is not the same claim as the real, unswapped rule actually reaching one. Nobody has proven that. This proof says three is alone in one narrow sense: among Mersenne primes, it is the only leading number the heuristic favors. It says nothing about whether the heuristic is even the right way to think about the real sequence in the first place.

Where it stands

Three is provably the only Mersenne prime whose swap-in predicts contraction under this heuristic. That is now closed. What remains open: the heuristic explaining why the swap-outs fail as easy substitutes was never a proof that the original, unmodified rule succeeds either. That's a different question, and it's still sitting there unanswered.
